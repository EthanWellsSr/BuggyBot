LLM Feasibility · Lesson 1

Why you can't train an LLM at home

You trained a 475,000-parameter sign classifier on your own workstation in about two minutes. A model that writes Python is the same idea scaled up so far that the scaling is the whole problem. Here is exactly where the wall is.

Anchor

Your GTSRB model: 162k parameters, ~39,000 images (~300 MB), trained in ~2 minutes on a CPU, hit ~95% accuracy. Hold that in mind — every number below is measured against it.

There are three walls, not one

"Too big" is vague. The real answer is three separate hard limits, and a home setup fails all three by a wide margin.

1. The compute wall — the sheer number of calculations

Training cost scales with parameters × data. Meta's Llama 3.1 405B model took 30.8 million H100 GPU-hours1 — about 3,500 GPU-years of compute, run on ~16,000 GPUs for ~100 days, at an estimated ~$60 million.2 On a single machine that same job would take on the order of millennia. Your 2-minute CPU run and a $60M datacenter run are not on the same chart.

2. The memory wall — it has to fit to train at all

To train a model, its weights plus gradients plus optimizer state must live in GPU memory at once. The rule of thumb is ~16–20 bytes per parameter during training.3 Do the arithmetic:

  • Your model: 162k × 16 ≈ 2.6 MB. Fits on a phone.
  • A "small" 7B LLM: 7B × 16 ≈ 112 GB. A high-end consumer GPU has 24 GB — it doesn't fit.
  • 405B: ≈ 8 TB. No single GPU on earth holds that; it's split across thousands.

This is the wall people underestimate: even a modest LLM overflows a home GPU before training can even start.

3. The data wall — the model is only as big as what it read

Your classifier learned from 39,000 labeled images you downloaded in one zip. Llama 3 was trained on roughly 15 trillion tokens of text — tens of terabytes, scraped, filtered, de-duplicated, and cleaned by a dedicated team. Gathering and curating that corpus is its own multi-million-dollar effort, separate from the training compute.

That's roughly 2.5 million× more parameters — and compute, data, and cost scale up together, not one at a time.

So what can you do at home?

The skeleton you already know still applies — you just don't start from zero:

  • Use a pretrained model via API — someone else paid the $60M; you call the finished model. This is the practical path to a working tool.
  • Fine-tune a small open model (LoRA) — freeze the giant pretrained model and train a tiny add-on layer. Because you touch a fraction of the parameters, it fits on one consumer GPU.4 This is where "at home" becomes real.
  • Train a toy model (nanoGPT) — same 5-step skeleton, transformer layers, runs on your workstation. Great for understanding; it produces Python-shaped gibberish, not a usable tool.

The dividing line: training a capable LLM from scratch is out; adapting or using one is in.

Name the three walls that stop home LLM training.

Compute (calculations), memory (fitting in VRAM), and data (corpus scale).

Why does even a 7B "small" LLM fail on a 24 GB consumer GPU?

Training needs ~16–20 bytes/parameter, so 7B needs ~112 GB — it can't fit to train.

What makes LoRA fine-tuning feasible at home when full training isn't?

You freeze the huge model and train only a tiny add-on, so far fewer parameters fit on one GPU.
